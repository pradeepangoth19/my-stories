Leaning on Shadows

Its not that what we think ! It is what we are actually !! If it come to end like now or never i'll choose now and forever!!!

By Angoth Devi Pradeep ·

You poured yourself into them like water into a cracked vessel, never noticing the leak because you were too busy trying to keep them full. You gave them your secrets, the ones that taste like copper in the back of your throat, and you gave them your time—the only thing you can never get back. You sat across from them at dinner, looking at their face, and you didn't just see a person; you saw the next fifty years. You saw the porch, the quiet nights, the way your hands would look when they were wrinkled and thin. You had already lived a thousand lives with them in your head.

And while you were building that cathedral of a future, they were out there, quietly dismantling the foundation.

The betrayal doesn't come like a thunderclap. It comes like a cold draft under a locked door. It’s the way they started holding their phone a little tighter, the way their eyes stayed on the screen a second too long, or the way they started picking fights just to create a reason to leave the room. You felt it. That sickening, cold intuition in the pit of your stomach that told you something was wrong. But you choked that feeling down. You called it "insecurity." You told yourself you were being "crazy" because you trusted them more than you trusted your own eyes. You loved them so much you were willing to lie to yourself just to keep their image perfect.

Then the truth broke. It wasn't a mistake. It wasn't a "moment of weakness." It was a series of choices. Every text they sent, every lie they told to your face while you were asking if they were okay—those were choices.

You want to ask if they were bored. You want to scream into the silence of your room, "Was I not enough?" You think if you could just understand the *why*, the pain would stop. But boredom is a coward’s excuse. They weren't bored of you; they were bored of the responsibility of being a good person. They liked the safety you gave them, the way you were always there to catch them, but they wanted the thrill of the hunt without losing the comfort of the home. They took your heart—the one thing you told them was fragile—and they used it as a stepping stone to get somewhere else.

It feels like a death, but there is no funeral. You are mourning a person who is still breathing, but who never actually existed. The person you loved wouldn't have done this to you. So now you’re left holding the pieces of a ghost. You look at your hands and realize they are shaking, not just from sadness, but from a raw, jagged anger that makes you want to tear the world apart. How could they sleep next to you? How could they look into your eyes and say "I love you" while the scent of someone else was still on their skin?

Real love isn't the "giving everything" you did. That was a sacrifice. Real love is a mutual protection. It’s supposed to be the one place in the world where you don't have to keep your guard up. When that is broken, the world becomes a loud, terrifying place.

You realize now that you shouldn't have been "everything" to them. You should have been a partner, not a parent, not a savior, and not a safety net. You realize that by giving them the power to destroy you, you forgot to keep the power to save yourself.

The struggle now isn't just about losing them. It’s about the fact that every time you close your eyes, you see that imagined future—the one with the hand-holding and the growing old—and you have to watch it burn, over and over again. You have to learn how to stand on your own legs when you’ve spent years leaning on a shadow. It’s raw, it’s ugly, and it leaves scars that itch when the weather changes. But those scars are the only thing you have left that is truly yours. They are the proof that you survived the person who promised they would never hurt you.

The war doesn't stay on the outside; it moves into your bones. It’s the 3:00 AM ceiling-stare where your brain becomes a courtroom, replaying every conversation, every look, and every "I’ll be home late" to see where the lie began. You become a detective of your own misery, digging through old photos and texts, looking for the exact second their heart shifted. You want to find the moment the rot started so you can cut it out, but the more you dig, the more you realize you were being played for a fool while you were being a saint.

The anger is a hot, oily thing that sits in your chest. It makes you want to reach out and break something of theirs, or better yet, break the silence. You want to send that text. You want to demand they feel even a fraction of the hollow, echoing void they left inside you. But you know, deep down, that they aren't feeling it. While you are drowning in the "why," they are breathing just fine. That’s the sharpest part of the blade: the realization that while you were making them your entire world, you were just a chapter in theirs.

You start to hate your own kindness. You look in the mirror and despise the person who was so "stupid" to believe in a forever. You feel a frantic need to become cold, to become hard, to never let a single soul see the soft parts of you again. You tell yourself that trust is a luxury you can no longer afford. But that’s the internal trap—if you let their betrayal turn you into stone, then they’ve won. They didn’t just take your past and your future; they took your ability to be human.

Every song, every street corner, every mundane smell of a certain laundry detergent becomes a landmine. You’ll be fine for three hours, and then a specific shade of sunlight will hit the floor and remind you of a morning when you felt safe in their arms, and the grief will hit you like a physical blow to the stomach. You find yourself gasping for air in the middle of a grocery store because you saw the cereal they like. It’s pathetic. It’s exhausting.

You struggle with the urge to check their social media, to see if they look as miserable as you feel. And when you see them smiling, or worse, see them moving on as if you were a discarded habit, the war turns inward. You start to wonder if you were the problem. Maybe you were too much. Maybe you were too boring. Maybe your "all" wasn't what they wanted. You have to fight that thought every single day. You have to scream at yourself that someone’s inability to be faithful is a reflection of their character, not your worth.

But the hardest part of the war is the silence. The silence of the phone that used to buzz. The silence of the side of the bed that’s now cold. You’re learning to live with a ghost who didn’t have the decency to leave. You’re waiting for a version of them to come back and apologize, to fix it, to tell you it was all a nightmare. But that person never existed. You fell in love with a mask, and now you have to figure out who you are when you’re not busy loving a lie.

The realization doesn’t hit you like a wave; it hits you like a floor falling out from under your feet. You keep waiting for a moment of cinematic closure—a tearful confession, a grand apology, or a moment where they look at you and finally understand the magnitude of the soul they shattered. You think that if you can just find the right words, you can make them feel the weight of what they did. You want to see them bleed emotionally, just a little, so you know it was real for them too.

But the bitter reality is the silence. Or worse, the indifference.

You look at them and realize they aren't the person you built that future for. That person was a ghost, a character you co-authored with a liar. The person standing in front of you is a stranger who happens to have the same face. They look at your tears not with remorse, but with annoyance. They look at your pain like it’s an inconvenience, a bill they don’t want to pay. They offer hollow excuses that sound like they were read off a script—"I didn't mean to," "It just happened," "I was confused."

Every word they say is a fresh insult to your intelligence. You realize they aren't sorry they hurt you; they are only sorry they got caught. They are sorry they have to deal with the consequences of your grief.

The most jagged pill to swallow is that there is no "justice" coming. The world doesn't stop for your broken heart. They might go on to be happy. They might take the lessons they learned from breaking you and use them to be better for the next person. That is the ultimate unfairness—that you were the training ground for their growth, and all you got in return was a scorched earth. You feel like a burnt-out building while they walk away into a new city.

You realize that your trust wasn't a gift to them; it was a weapon you handed them, and they didn't hesitate to use it. You remember all the times you felt that "hunch," that tiny voice in your head saying *something is wrong*, and you remember how you strangled that voice to death because you wanted to believe in "us." The betrayal you feel toward yourself is almost worse than the betrayal you feel toward them. You let yourself be a victim because you thought sacrifice was the same thing as love.

It sinks in that the future you saw—the one with the shared house, the old age, the unshakable bond—was a solo performance. You were the only one building the house; they were just staying in it for free until they found somewhere else to go. You weren't a team; you were a host, and they were a parasite.

This is the end of the "why." You stop asking why they did it because the answer is finally clear: they did it because they could. They did it because they valued a moment of newness over a lifetime of history. They did it because your heart was a price they were willing to pay.

And as you sit in the ruins of the trust you gave so freely, you realize that closure isn't something they give you. Closure is the moment you stop waiting for them to care and realize that their lack of care is the only answer you're ever going to get. You are left with the cold, hard truth: you loved a version of them that never existed, and they loved a version of themselves that you made possible. Now, both of those people are dead.

The fluorescent lights of the grocery store are too bright, humming with a low-frequency buzz that vibrates in your teeth. You are standing in Aisle 4, staring at a box of cereal—the specific brand they liked, the one you always made sure was in the pantry.

You remember a Saturday morning, six months ago, when the light hit the kitchen floor in a specific shade of gold. They had laughed, messy-haired and seemingly soft, and you felt so safe in their arms that you leaned your entire weight into them. You didn't know then that you were leaning on a shadow. You didn't see that while you were planning where the photos would go in a house you hadn't bought yet, they were already holding their phone a little tighter, their eyes drifting toward a different exit.

Now, the silence of the aisle is heavy. You realize you are holding the box so hard the cardboard is beginning to buckle under your thumb. You aren't just mourning a person; you are mourning the version of yourself that stood in that golden kitchen light and believed in "forever".

You put the cereal back on the shelf. The air in the store feels thin, but as you walk toward the exit, you notice the weight of being "everything" start to shift. You are just a person in a store, buying bread for one, finally learning how to stand on your own legs.

The internal war ends not with a victory, but with a ceasefire. You finally stop trying to force a broken person to see your value. You realize that pouring a gallon of love into a thimble-sized heart will always result in a mess on the floor, and that isn't the fault of the water—it’s the capacity of the vessel.

You choose to be strong not by hardening your heart, but by protecting your energy. You show up for the family and friends who actually see you, wearing your strength like a coat of armor that is heavy but necessary. You walk away from the wreckage not because you are running, but because there is nothing left to guard in the ruins.

The future is no longer a vivid, painted mural of two people; it is a blank, quiet room. It’s intimidating, but for the first time in a long time, the air is clean. You aren't waiting for anyone. You aren't supporting a weight that isn't yours. You are simply breathing. And in that breath, you find the only person whose trust you actually need to win back: Yourself.

You stand at the edge of the life you tried to build, looking back at the versions of yourself you wore like costumes. You tried to be their strength when they were weak, their parent when they were lost, and their anchor when they wanted to drift. You stretched your soul until it was thin, trying to be everything they demanded, only to realize that to them, you weren't a person—you were a service.

The bitter truth settles in: if you have to become someone else to be loved, the person they love isn't you. And the moment you stopped being able to play those roles, the moment you showed your own exhaustion or your own needs, they treated you like a broken tool. They walked away to find a new stage and a new actor, leaving behind the only thing that was ever real.

They are trapped in a circle, chasing the "next" thing because they don't know how to keep the "true" thing. They move from one person to another, wasting hearts like spent matches, never realizing that the mess they are running from is inside themselves. They left you behind because they couldn't afford the price of your depth.

But as you turn away from the wreckage, you feel a strange, heavy weight lift off your shoulders. It is the weight of being "everything."

You realize now that you don't need to be their father, their mother, or their savior. You just need to be yourself. You stop waiting for them to come back and apologize, because their return would only mean putting the costume back on. Instead, you walk into your own future, solitary but solid.

You were the one who was capable of staying. You were the one who was real. They might have wasted what was meant for them, but they didn't waste you—they just lost the privilege of having you. You aren't "nothing" because you couldn't fulfill their impossible roles. You are finally, for the first time, everything you were actually meant to be.

The story ends here. Not with a "we," but with a "You."
